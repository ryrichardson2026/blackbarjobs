Who Needs a Texas Security License?

In Texas, anyone who works as a paid security officer — whether armed or unarmed — is required to hold a valid commission issued by the Texas Department of Public Safety (DPS) through its Private Security Bureau. This applies regardless of whether you're employed full-time, part-time, or on a per-diem basis.

The commission is sometimes called a "guard card," though that term isn't official Texas DPS terminology. The formal credential is a Security Officer Commission — Level II for unarmed officers and Level III for armed officers.

Working security without a valid commission is a Class A misdemeanor in Texas. Most employers will verify your commission status before your first shift, and many won't schedule you at all until it's confirmed in the DPS system.

Important

Texas does not recognize security officer licenses from other states. If you're relocating to Texas from another state, you'll need to complete Texas-specific training and apply for a Texas commission even if you're currently licensed elsewhere.

Level II vs Level III — What's the Difference?

Texas issues two primary levels of security officer commission. The level you need depends on whether the role you're pursuing requires you to carry a firearm.

Unarmed
Level II — Security Officer Commission

The standard unarmed security officer commission. Required for all paid security work where the officer does not carry a firearm. Covers the vast majority of security jobs in Texas — corporate campuses, retail, healthcare, residential, and most commercial sites.

  • 30 hours of classroom training from a DPS-approved school
  • Written examination at an approved testing site
  • Background check and fingerprinting
  • DPS application and commission fee
  • No firearm qualification required
Armed
Level III — Armed Security Officer Commission

The armed security officer commission. Required for any paid security role where the officer carries a handgun. Builds on the Level II commission — you must hold or complete Level II training before obtaining Level III.

  • Level II commission (or concurrent completion of Level II training)
  • Additional firearms training — minimum 40 hours
  • Range qualification — demonstrated proficiency with the approved handgun
  • Separate DPS armed commission application and fee
  • Ongoing range qualification required at renewal
Which Level Do Most DFW Jobs Require?

The majority of security job openings in the Dallas–Fort Worth area — corporate campuses, retail, healthcare, warehouses, residential communities, and most event venues — require only a Level II commission. Level III is required for roles specifically designated as armed, which tend to be at financial institutions, data centers, high-value logistics sites, and certain government-contracted positions.

Basic Eligibility Requirements

To be eligible for a Texas security officer commission, applicants must meet the following baseline criteria set by the Texas DPS Private Security Bureau:

  • Be at least 18 years of age
  • Be a United States citizen or lawfully admitted alien
  • Have not been convicted of a felony or Class A or Class B misdemeanor within the past 20 years
  • Have not been convicted of any crime involving moral turpitude
  • Be of good moral character
  • Not be currently on deferred adjudication for a felony or Class A or Class B misdemeanor
  • Not be prohibited by state or federal law from possessing a firearm (Level III applicants)
  • Complete the required training from a DPS-licensed training school
  • Pass the required written examination
  • Pass a criminal background check and fingerprinting

A high school diploma or GED is not formally required by DPS for the commission itself, though individual employers may require it as a condition of employment.

Step-by-Step: How to Get Your Texas Security License

The process from start to licensed officer typically takes 2–4 weeks, depending on how quickly you can schedule training and how fast DPS processes your application.

1
Find a DPS-Licensed Training School
Texas requires that your pre-licensing training be completed through a school licensed by the DPS Private Security Bureau. Schools offer in-person, online, or hybrid formats. Costs range from $75–$200 depending on the provider and format. Search the DPS website for the current list of licensed schools in your area.
~1–3 days to research and enroll
2
Complete 30 Hours of Level II Training
The Level II pre-licensing course covers Texas security law, emergency procedures, communication and report writing, public relations, and first aid basics. Some schools complete all 30 hours in a single weekend; others spread across weekday evenings. Upon completion you'll receive a certificate of training.
2–7 days depending on school schedule
3
Pass the Written Examination
After completing training, you'll take a written exam administered through a DPS-approved testing provider. The exam covers material from your training course. Most candidates pass on the first attempt with adequate course preparation. You must pass before submitting your DPS application.
Same day or within a few days of training completion
4
Submit Fingerprints
Texas requires electronic fingerprinting as part of the background check process. Fingerprinting is done through IdentoGO (MorphoTrust), which has locations throughout the DFW metro. Schedule an appointment online — walk-in availability varies. Fingerprint results are submitted directly to DPS.
Appointment typically available within a few days
5
Apply Online Through the DPS Private Security Portal
Submit your commission application through the Texas DPS Private Security online portal (psp.txdps.state.tx.us). You'll upload your training certificate, exam results, and pay the application fee. DPS processes applications in the order received — processing times vary but typically run 1–3 weeks.
Processing: 1–3 weeks
6
Receive Your Commission Card
Once approved, DPS will mail your physical commission card and your commission will be active in the DPS verification system. Your employer can verify your status online before your card arrives — so you may be able to start work before the physical card is in your hand, depending on the employer's policy.
Commission active upon DPS approval
Pro Tip

Many security employers in DFW will conditionally hire you while your application is pending — particularly if you have your training certificate and fingerprints submitted. Ask the employer directly whether they can start you during the DPS processing window.

Renewal & Continuing Education

Texas security officer commissions are not permanent — they expire and must be renewed on a regular schedule to remain valid. Working on an expired commission carries the same legal exposure as working without one.

Level II Renewal

Level II commissions are valid for two years from the date of issue. To renew, officers must complete 6 hours of continuing education (CE) from a DPS-licensed provider and submit a renewal application with the applicable fee through the DPS portal before the expiration date.

Level III Renewal

Level III armed commissions also renew on a two-year cycle and require the same 6 hours of CE as the Level II renewal, plus a current firearms range qualification demonstrating continued proficiency. Officers who let their range qualification lapse must re-qualify before their armed commission can be renewed.

Late Renewal

DPS allows a grace period for late renewals, but working security during an expired commission period is not permitted. Officers who miss their renewal window should not work until the renewal is processed. Employers are responsible for verifying active commission status before scheduling officers.

Set a Reminder

Mark your commission expiration date and set a reminder 90 days out. CE courses fill up and DPS processing takes time — starting the renewal process late is one of the most common reasons officers have a gap in active commission status.

Disqualifying Offenses

Texas DPS will deny a security commission application based on criminal history. The following are common disqualifying factors — this list is not exhaustive, and DPS evaluates each application individually based on the nature, recency, and pattern of any criminal history.

  • Conviction of any felony offense
  • Conviction of a Class A or Class B misdemeanor within the past 20 years
  • Deferred adjudication for a felony or Class A/B misdemeanor that is still active
  • Any conviction involving moral turpitude (fraud, theft, dishonesty-related offenses)
  • Family violence convictions (bars possession of a firearm under federal law — disqualifying for Level III)
  • Outstanding warrants or pending criminal charges at time of application
  • Prior revocation of a Texas security commission

Class C misdemeanors (traffic violations, minor infractions) generally do not disqualify an applicant on their own, but a pattern of Class C offenses may be evaluated as part of the overall character assessment.

Not Sure If You Qualify?

DPS offers a pre-application criminal history evaluation for individuals who are uncertain about their eligibility. This allows you to get a preliminary assessment before investing in training costs. Contact the DPS Private Security Bureau directly for guidance on the pre-evaluation process.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to get a Texas security license? +
The full process — training, exam, fingerprinting, and DPS processing — typically takes 2–4 weeks for most applicants. Training itself can be completed in as little as a weekend at schools that offer intensive formats. DPS application processing is the primary variable; it typically runs 1–3 weeks depending on application volume.
Can I work security while my application is being processed? +
Technically no — you need an active commission to legally work as a paid security officer in Texas. However, some employers will conditionally onboard you, complete HR paperwork, and assign you to orientation or training assignments while waiting for DPS to finalize your commission. Ask the employer directly about their policy during the application window.
Do I need a high school diploma to get a Texas security license? +
Texas DPS does not formally require a high school diploma or GED as a condition of the commission itself. However, many employers — especially those with corporate campus or healthcare contracts — do require a diploma or equivalent as a condition of employment. Check the specific job posting requirements rather than assuming DPS standards are the only threshold.
Will a misdemeanor disqualify me from getting a Texas security license? +
It depends on the class and recency of the offense. Class A and Class B misdemeanor convictions within the past 20 years are disqualifying. Class C misdemeanors (such as minor traffic violations) generally do not disqualify on their own. If you have a misdemeanor on your record, consider requesting a pre-application criminal history evaluation from the DPS Private Security Bureau before investing in training costs.
Can I get my Texas security license online? +
The DPS application itself is submitted online through the Private Security Portal. Some training schools offer online or hybrid pre-licensing courses that satisfy the 30-hour classroom requirement. However, fingerprinting must be completed in person at an IdentoGO location, and Level III firearms qualification must be done at an approved range — those components cannot be completed online.
Does Texas accept security licenses from other states? +
No. Texas does not have reciprocity agreements with other states for security officer commissions. If you're relocating to Texas with an out-of-state security license, you'll need to complete Texas-specific training through a DPS-licensed school and apply for a Texas commission from scratch.
How much does it cost to get a Texas security guard card? +
Total costs for a Level II (unarmed) commission typically run $140–$310, covering training, exam, fingerprinting, and the DPS application fee. Level III (armed) adds an estimated $240–$510 in additional costs for firearms training, range qualification, and the armed commission fee. Some employers reimburse training costs upon hire — worth asking before you pay out of pocket.
