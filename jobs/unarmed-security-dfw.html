Role Overview

What Does an Unarmed Security Officer Do?

Unarmed security officers provide a visible deterrent and safety presence across a wide range of environments. Common assignments include office buildings, apartment complexes, retail centers, hospitals, schools, warehouses, and event venues. No firearm is carried — primary tools are a radio, ID badge, and incident report forms.

Day-to-day responsibilities focus on access control, patrol, monitoring surveillance systems, and documenting activity. Unarmed officers frequently serve as the first point of contact for visitors, employees, and the public — requiring strong communication and de-escalation skills.

Unarmed roles are the most common entry point into the security industry. Many employers will train on the job and sponsor guard card licensing for qualified candidates.

Duties

Common Duties & Responsibilities

  • Patrol assigned areas on foot or by vehicle
  • Monitor access points and control entry/exit
  • Check IDs and maintain visitor logs
  • Monitor surveillance cameras and alarm systems
  • Respond to disturbances, incidents, and emergencies
  • Write daily activity and incident reports
  • Assist visitors, employees, and the public
  • Enforce site rules and company policies
  • Coordinate with law enforcement when required
Requirements

Training & Credential Requirements

Valid Texas Security License (Level II) — required or obtainable
Clean criminal background check
High school diploma or GED
CPR / First Aid certification (preferred by most employers)
Valid driver's license (required for patrol roles)

Requirements vary by employer and site. Verify directly with the hiring company.

Experience

Experience Requirements

  • Many unarmed roles are open to candidates with no prior security experience — guard card is the primary requirement
  • Customer service, retail, or hospitality experience is frequently cited as relevant by employers
  • Military or law enforcement background is an advantage but not required for most unarmed positions
  • Physical requirements typically include extended standing, walking patrol routes, and remaining alert during long shifts
  • Strong verbal communication and de-escalation skills are consistently valued
Schedules

Typical Schedules & Work Environments

  • Day shifts common at office buildings, hospitals, schools, and retail centers
  • Overnight shifts available at warehouses, apartment complexes, and industrial sites
  • Weekend and holiday coverage frequently required
  • 8 and 12-hour shifts both common depending on employer
  • Indoor assignments at lobbies, reception desks, and control rooms
  • Outdoor patrol in parking lots, perimeters, and construction sites
  • Part-time and PRN (as-needed) positions widely available
